Currency

Pay attention to your attention.

How many exchange rates will you check on this trip?

How many times will you ask what something costs, and then decide if it is worth it?

When did you last ask the same questions about the thing you spend all day, every day?

The posture

Two currencies, one of them unwatched

On this trip you will handle the Turkish lira, the Egyptian pound and the Moroccan dirham. You will learn what each is worth against the others. You will divide your money across days, invest it in meals and moments, and hope it pays off. You will notice, right away, if someone shortchanges you.

You carry a second currency that you spend far more often, and almost never look at.

Money

Concrete, so we watch it

  • We set a budget
  • We divide it across days
  • We compare rates
  • We invest it and hope for a return
  • We notice when it is wasted or taken
Attention

Abstract, so we lose track

  • We praise it and rarely plan it
  • It divides itself all day
  • We seldom ask what it buys
  • We invest it by habit and hope
  • We rarely notice what it cost

Attention is the more fundamental of the two. It is finite, you get a fresh supply each morning, and whatever you spend it on becomes your experience. Many of the other postures we practice depend on it: curiosity, spectral thinking, being a thermostat instead of a thermometer. Each one is a decision about where to put your attention.

So the posture for this seminar is Currency. Notice where yours goes, why it goes there, and what it buys.

Why it matters

Someone else is already counting

Money is not the only reason to pay attention to attention. Your attention is also somebody else's revenue. Consider what the numbers say.

Advertising is sold by the minute of your attention, and the most valuable products on earth are built to collect it. The economist Herbert Simon saw this coming in 1971: "a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention."

If your attention were a paycheck, would you hand it over without looking at who was collecting?

That is not a reason to feel guilty, or to throw away your phone. It is a reason to spend your attention on purpose, at least some of the time. A trip like this is a rare chance to practice.

A way to remember

The four T's

A ledger needs only a few columns. Here are four, each starting with the same letter, so you can carry them in your head. They are also the questions you will ask yourself at night.

  1. T
    TrackWhere did my attention go today? Name it, without judging it.
  2. T
    TraceWhy did it go there? Did I choose it, or did something pull it? Who gained from where it went?
  3. T
    TradeWhat did I trade away? What might I have missed because of where it went? A guess is a good answer.
  4. T
    TreasureWhat is one moment I want to keep, and why did it matter?

Track, Trace, Trade, Treasure. If you remember nothing else from this page, remember those four words.

What it buys

Trade expectations for presence

Most of us arrive somewhere having already spent our attention. We pay in advance for the place we imagined: the photographs, the stories, the way we think a bazaar, a mosque or a medina should look. Then we spend the trip checking what is in front of us against what we paid for.

Expectations are a kind of prepaid purchase. They make us look for what we expect, and they make us pass over what we did not budget for. Presence is the other trade. You give your attention to what is actually here, even when it is plainer, stranger, noisier or more ordinary than you thought it should be.

What you came to see matters less than what is actually there.

You do not have to give up your expectations. You only have to notice when they are doing your looking for you.

Attention as a tool, and attention for its own sake

There is a quieter version of the same trap. Much of the attention we spend on a trip is used to maneuver: to keep to the schedule, to get the photograph, to find the right place, to say the right thing, to meet what is expected of a thoughtful traveler. That attention is useful, and you will need it. But it is spent only on getting through, and it asks one question of everything it meets: what do I do with this?

Attention for its own sake asks nothing in return. It is simply being with what is in front of you, with no photograph to take, no lesson to extract and no impression to make. Not every expenditure has to pay off. Some of the best are given freely.

Attention that is only a means to something else will miss most of what it passes.

What did I expect to find here, and what is actually here?

Where am I attending to what should be, rather than what is?

When today did I use my attention only to manage the day, and when did I simply attend, for no reason at all?

Before we land in Istanbul

Three short prompts

Write a few sentences on each in your own journal, before we land in Istanbul.

What do I already think I know about Türkiye, Egypt and Morocco, and where did those ideas come from?

Those ideas are filters. Your attention will use them without asking you.

What am I most eager to dig into on this trip?

Hold it loosely. Then notice what the place offers instead.

Where do I expect my attention to go, and what do I think it might skip?

While we travel

A few minutes a night

Most evenings, write in your own journal. Use the four T's each night so patterns can show up over time. A line or two each is plenty. Skip a night without guilt.

These entries are not homework to hand in. They are the raw material for your mid-trip synthesis in Egypt, so the more honestly you capture what you noticed, the more you will have to work with there.

Track: What did I pay attention to today?

Write down what was actually there, not what you expected or thought should be.

Trace: Why did my attention go there?

Did I choose it, or did it pull me?

Trade: What might I have missed because of where my attention went?

A guess is a good answer. Nobody can see exactly what they did not notice.

Treasure: One moment to keep, and why it mattered.

A sample reflection

Made up, only to show the feel. Yours can be shorter, messier or longer.

TrackThe smell of cardamom, a man restacking oranges into a perfect pyramid, three languages at once. Also my phone, more than I would like.

TraceThe oranges pulled me. I stopped without deciding to. The phone was habit.

TradeI was scanning for what looked old and "authentic," so I walked past the bread cart. Claire pointed out the vendor's daughter doing the arithmetic in her head. I never saw her.

TreasureThe orange pyramid. Someone took that much care over something that would be sold within the hour.

A few more prompts

If a night feels flat, try one of these as well.

  • What did you expect to find today, and what did the place offer instead?
  • Who decided what you were shown today? What might that arrangement leave out?
  • When were you bored, uncomfortable or confused today? What did you do with that feeling?
  • Where was your attention while someone was speaking to you? On them, or on your reply?
  • What did your phone take from the day, and what did it give?
  • What did you notice today that was of no use to you at all? Why did it hold you?
  • What was worth your attention today, and did it get it?
Mid-trip, Egypt

Bring this to your synthesis

Your nightly journaling, starting in Istanbul, is setting the stage for the mid-trip synthesis in Egypt. Think of it as your ledger: by then you will have days of entries showing where your attention went and what it bought. Before the synthesis, skim your journal. You do not need to reread all of it. As you read, notice what repeats and what is missing, then jot a few lines on these.

What do I keep noticing?

When was my attention chosen, and when did something pull it?

What might my attention be skipping? Who could tell me?

Where did my expectations do my looking for me, and where was I present to what was actually there?

Where do I want to aim my attention for the rest of the trip?

A calling

Spend it well

Across these days you will notice where your attention goes. It goes to many things: the loud, the new, the frightening, the familiar, whatever happens to be in front of you. None of that is a failing.

Attention can also be aimed, and it can be given to what is here, not only to what we expected. It is one of the few things you can give that cannot be given twice. When you give it fully to a person, a place or a purpose, you tell them they matter.

"Don't you think maybe they are the same thing? Love and attention?"

Sister Sarah Joan, in Lady Bird (2017, written and directed by Greta Gerwig)

Ponder this on your own, in whatever quiet moments the trip gives you.

If you spent your attention on what you love, would that be the most meaningful investment you could make?
